Finding this restaurant for our lunch was a bit of challenge, taking a taxi from Kawaramachi station and getting a bit lost within the sea of nondescript buildings. However, once we keyed in on the restaurant with its blue demarcations, we walked into a wet stone path, took of our shoes, and were greeted by a bowing okami. From there, we were led into our private dining room, where it became unclear whether or not we were the only ones dining at the time. Luckily, the tatami-matted room had zaisu for our legs, with no need to cross the whole meal.

We were welcomed with a nice cup of green tea ***(3/5)***. 

Our starting dish was a tofu formed into the shape of a wave with okra and junsai. Once we dug into it with a spoon, it's evident how precisely and technically sliced it was in order to attain its shape. The bed of ponzu was light and the gelatinous texture of the okra and junsai were interesting ***(4/5)***.

The next dish was described as one of Nakamura's most famous; a piping hot mustard miso soup with grilled mochi. The mustard was just strong so as to not sting the nose, while the mochi itself was a tad flavorless. Much of the enjoyment came towards the end when using the mochi to soak up the mustard flavor ***(2.5/5)***.

The sashimi dish was relatively standard. For the striped jack, hamo (conger eel), and prawn respectively, we were given soy sauce mixed with dark soy sauce, a light oil, and a thicker soy sauce and dashi. The striped jack had a touch of spice for seasoning. The prawn seemed very lightly blanched while the conger eel had an interesting texture ***(3/5)***. 

We then had a seabass dish with sliced green beans inside of a chili pepper shell and shishito peppers dusted in bonito. The seabass for me was a tad dry and subtle flavor-wise on its own, but the green vinegar sauce imparted good amounts of tanginess and moisture. I also enjoyed the sesame on the green beans ***(3.5/5)***.

For soup, we had the hamu again except with various vegetables including eggplant, mushroom, and calabash. The dashi broth was delicious while the hamu, with a more delicate flavor, took on a spongier texture and broke apart easily with chopsticks ***(3/5)***. 

Next, we had a delicate, ornate dish that we were instructed to mix: sea urchin, grated yam, wasabi, and seaweed. The sliminess and coolness of the dish was surprisingly refreshing, and the brininess evoked the theme of the ocean. The sea urchin was small, but fresh ***(3.5/5)***.

Here is where some headscratchers began. The Japanese tilefish, one of their other famous dishes, came with skin and scales, which we were instructed to leave for the following soup. The initial aroma of the sake was enticing, but further bites became a bit of a chore. The skin itself was also an obstacle when eating, not to mention the bones ***(2/5)***.

Then, in non-photogenic fashion, dashi broth was poured over our tilefish remains. The dashi broth itself was decent and savory ***(2.5/5)***.

In kaiseki fashion, we were then given an assortment of Japanese pickles with ginger rice and the first of two cups of roasted tea. The cucumber was tangy and the radish was more subtle, having a bit of a chewier texture. The pickled kelp was a bit too salty and soy fermented for my liking, but the ginger rice grew on me and imparted small notes of spiciness that saved the dish ***(3/5)***.

Onto dessert, there was a solid but not particularly inventive matcha dish. Vanilla ice cream was shrouded by matcha while various chilled mochis and red beans flanked the center. Overall, not too sweet ***(3/5)***.

We finished with a final cup of roasted tea ***(3.5/5)***.


## BBB

*02* was the standout dish for me. The evenness of the knife cuts, the subtlety of the ponzu flavors and tofu, as well as the usage of junsai and okra to add gelatin made this the highlight and a real epitome of the food here. Artistically assembled, it showed Isshisoden Nakamura's more traditional Japanese ingredients and technique with interesting textures at the forefront over bold, in-your-face flavors.
